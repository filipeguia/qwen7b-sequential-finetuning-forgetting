Henriette was a hurricane that formed well southwest of the Baja California Peninsula 
and did not bring any direct impacts to land. The system briefly became a remnant low as 
it approached and crossed into the central Pacific basin, but later reorganized and 
intensified into a Category 1 hurricane as it passed well north of Hawaii.

BEST TRACK 
 
 
The “best track2” positions and intensities for Hurricane Henriette are listed in Table 1. The 
“best track” chart of Henriette’s path is given in Fig. 1, with the wind and pressure histories along 
with available observations3 shown in Figs. 2 and 3, respectively.  
There were no ship observations of tropical-storm-force winds associated with Henriette. 
Origin 
Henriette originated from a tropical wave that moved off the coast of Africa on 16 July and 
crossed Central America around 28–30 July. The system emerged over the eastern Pacific and 
continued westward for a few days, eventually evolving into a broad area of low pressure while 
producing disorganized convection. Showers and thunderstorms associated with this system 
gradually became better organized when the disturbance was located well southwest of the Baja 
California Peninsula on 3 August. It is estimated that the system became a tropical depression at 
0600 UTC 4 August, when scatterometer data revealed a well-defined surface circulation and 
deep convection consolidated over the center. The cyclone strengthened into a 35-kt tropical 
storm about 6 h later. 
Peak Intensity and Minimum Pressure 
Henriette’s estimated peak intensity of 75 kt around 1200 UTC 11 August is based on a 
blend of subjective Dvorak intensity estimates from HFO, JTWC, and SAB, along with objective 
estimates from UW-CIMSS. The estimated minimum central pressure of 986 mb is based 
primarily on the Knaff–Zehr–Courtney (KZC) pressure–wind relationship. It should be noted that

FORECAST AND WARNING VERIFICATION 
 
Table 2 provides the number of hours in advance of formation with the first NHC Tropical 
Weather Outlook (TWO) forecast in each likelihood category. The tropical wave that eventually 
became Henriette was introduced in the TWO with sufficient lead time, with low probabilities of 
formation (<30%) in the 2- and 7-day periods about 84 h and 144 h prior to genesis, respectively. 
These probabilities were gradually increased as the system began to consolidate and become 
more organized, with high formation probabilities (>60%) introduced about 30 h prior to genesis 
in the 2-day period and about 96 h prior in the 7-day period. Composites of the 7-day TWO 
genesis areas (Fig. 4) show that the spatial extent of the genesis region remained consistent 
through time, with the medium (40–60%) and high (>60%) probability contours centered very near 
the genesis location. As a result, the forecasts of Henriette’s formation location were highly 
accurate, with the cyclone forming near the center of the depicted genesis areas, yielding a 100% 
hit rate. It should also be noted that while Henriette weakened into a post-tropical low and then 
re-intensified in the central Pacific, NHC continued to issue official forecasts, maintaining forecast 
continuity while the system did not meet the criteria of a tropical cyclone. 
A verification of the NHC official track forecasts for Henriette is provided in Tables 3a and 
5a covering the eastern and central North Pacific basins, respectively. In both basins, the official 
track forecast errors were lower than the mean official errors from the previous 5-year period at 
all forecast times. A homogeneous comparison of the official track errors with selected guidance 
models is provided in Tables 3b and 5b. Several models outperformed the official NHC forecast 
through 36 h in the eastern Pacific; however, from 48 h through 120 h, the official forecasts were 
generally more accurate than the majority of the guidance. In the central Pacific, the official 
forecasts were typically more accurate than the guidance models at most forecast times.    
A verification of the NHC official intensity forecasts for Henriette is provided in Tables 4a 
and 6a covering the eastern and central North Pacific basins, respectively. In the eastern Pacific, 
the official intensity forecast errors were lower than the mean official errors from the previous 5-
year period at all forecast times. In the central Pacific, the official errors were lower than the 5-
year period from 60 h to 72 h. A homogeneous comparison of the official intensity errors with 
selected guidance models is provided in Tables 4b and 6b. Several models outperformed the 
official NHC forecasts during the 36–60 h forecast periods in the eastern Pacific. In the central
