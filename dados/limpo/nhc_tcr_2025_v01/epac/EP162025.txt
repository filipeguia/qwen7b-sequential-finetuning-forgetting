Hurricane Priscilla (EP162025), 4–10 October 2025

Priscilla was a category 3 hurricane (on the Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale) that moved along, but offshore of the coast of southwestern mainland Mexico and the Baja California Peninsula.

Hurricane Priscilla 4–10 OCTOBER 2025

## SYNOPTIC HISTORY

Priscilla developed from a monsoon trough that formed over the eastern Pacific around 30 September. Convection gradually increased over the subsequent days as a broad circulation drifted west-northwestward off the southern and southwestern Mexican coast. The surface low became better organized on the night of 3 October, with a sustained burst of deep convection within the broad inner core. Satellite wind observations later confirmed the development of a well-defined surface circulation, and a tropical depression formed at 1200 UTC 4 October about 260 n mi south-southwest of Manzanillo, Mexico. The “best track” chart of Priscilla’s path is given in Fig. 1, with the wind and pressure histories shown in Figs. 2 and 3, respectively. The best track positions and intensities are listed in Table 11. Priscilla became a tropical storm 6 h later on 4 October while slowly moving northwestward along the periphery of a weak mid-level ridge centered over Texas. The large tropical storm moved over warm sea-surface temperatures of around 29 to 30°C and through an environment with strong upper-level divergence. Despite its large size and moderate easterly vertical wind shear, Priscilla steadily strengthened through 5 October while turning north-northwestward and it became a hurricane at 0000 UTC 6 October while centered about 250 n mi south-southwest of Cabo Corrientes, Mexico. During the next 24 h, the hurricane showed little change in strength while it moved north-northwestward parallel to the coast of Mexico. Microwave and surface wind satellite data then indicated that, while the outer wind field remained rather broad, the inner core began to contract and the maximum winds steadily increased. Priscilla reached a peak intensity of 100 kt at 1800 UTC 7 October while located a couple of hundred n mi south-southwest of the southern tip of the Baja California Peninsula.

After reaching its peak intensity, Priscilla began a steady weakening trend as environmental conditions became increasingly unfavorable. On 8 October, satellite imagery showed a marked degradation of the cyclone’s inner core, with diminishing deep convection, warming cloud tops, and a poorly defined eye, and the system rapidly weakened from a major hurricane to a tropical storm. This weakening was attributed to decreasing sea-surface temperatures, increasing southwesterly vertical wind shear and increasing intrusions of dry air. Priscilla gradually lost organized deep convection during 9–10 October due to a continued unfavorable environment, causing the system to weaken to a low-end tropical storm by 10 October and a post-tropical remnant low at 0000 UTC 11 October. The remnant circulation continued to spin down and it dissipated by 0600 UTC 12 October, with the remaining upper-level tropical moisture transported northward into the southwestern United States in the flow ahead of an upper-level trough.

## METEOROLOGICAL STATISTICS

Observations in Priscilla (Figs. 2 and 3) include subjective satellite-based Dvorak technique intensity estimates from the Tropical Analysis and Forecast Branch (TAFB) and the Satellite Analysis Branch (SAB), objective Advanced Dvorak Technique (ADT) estimates and Satellite Consensus (SATCON) estimates from the Cooperative Institute for Meteorological Satellite Studies/University of Wisconsin-Madison (CIMSS/UW). Data and imagery from NOAA polar-orbiting satellites including the Advanced Microwave Sounding Unit (AMSU), the NASA Global Precipitation Mission (GPM), the European Space Agency’s Advanced Scatterometer (ASCAT), the Defense Meteorological Satellite Program (DMSP) satellites, and the Time-Resolved Observations of Precipitation structure and storm Intensity with a Constellation of Smallsats (TROPICS) satellites, among others, were also useful in constructing the best track of Priscilla.

Winds and Pressure Priscilla’s peak intensity of 100 kt at 1800 UTC 7 October was based on T5.5/102 kt subjective Dvorak satellite estimates from TAFB and SAB, along with DPRINT (DeeP IR INtensity of TCs) objective estimates from CIMSS/UW that ranged from 99–103 kt. Microwave imagery from ATMS at 2014 UTC prompted other objective estimates, including DMINT (Deep Multispectral INtensity of TCs estimator) and SATCON, to increase above 100 kt, although this information was not available at the 1800 UTC analysis time that day. While this peak represents only a 5-kt increase from the operational intensity assessment, it changes Priscilla’s designation to a major hurricane (Category 3 on the Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale). The minimum central pressure of 954 mb is based on the Knaff–Zehr–Courtney wind–pressure relationship.

A Mexican Naval weather station located on Socorro Island recorded a sustained wind of 47 knots (88 km/h) with a gust of 66 knots (122 km/h) when Priscilla's eye was about 60 n mi (115 km) to the north of that island. While tropical storm watches were issued for portions of mainland Mexico and the Baja Peninsula, no reports of sustained tropical-storm-force winds were recorded. Rainfall and Flooding Priscilla’s passage offshore of southwestern Mexico produced periods of heavy rainfall along the coast from 4–10 October (Fig. 4). Notable storm-total rainfall amounts included 13.24 inches (336.2 mm) at Socorro Island, 10.57 inches (268.4 mm) at Zihuatanejo in the state of Guerrero, and 10.55 inches (268.0 mm) at Melcor Ocampo in Michoacán. After Priscilla degenerated into a remnant low, southwesterly flow moved residual moisture over the Baja California peninsula, northwestern Mexico, and southwestern United States. This moisture, combined with the remnant moisture of Tropical Storm Raymond and a large upper-level trough over the western United States, contributed to flash flooding events across the region2.

## CASUALTY AND DAMAGE STATISTICS

There were no reports of casualties3 associated with Priscilla4. Localized damage from flooding and rough surf was reported near Puerto Vallarta and in Baja California Sur. Notable damage included 9 affected homes in Baja California Sur with 309 temporarily displaced individuals.

## FORECAST AND WARNING CRITIQUE

The genesis of Priscilla was well forecast (Table 2). The monsoonal low from which Priscilla developed was first introduced in the 7-day Tropical Weather Outlook 138 h before genesis. This likelihood was subsequently increased to the medium category 120 h before genesis and to the high category 96 h before formation. Lead times for the 2-day outlooks also demonstrated good reliability. A low probability was first indicated 84 h before genesis, with increases to medium and high probabilities at 60 h and 42 h before development, respectively. The location of Priscilla’s genesis was generally well forecast (Fig. 5). The ultimate genesis position fell on the western side of most of the low and medium probability genesis areas, indicating that the models and forecasts initially anticipated Priscilla to form slightly earlier.
