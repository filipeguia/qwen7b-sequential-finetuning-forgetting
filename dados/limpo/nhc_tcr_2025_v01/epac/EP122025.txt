Hurricane Lorena (EP122025), 2–4 September 2025

Lorena was a category 1 hurricane (on the Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale) that remained just offshore of the Baja California peninsula. While Lorena caused heavy rainfall and flooding over Baja California Sur, no fatalities or injuries were attributable to the cyclone.

Hurricane Lorena 2–4 SEPTEMBER 2025

## SYNOPTIC HISTORY

The genesis of Lorena can be traced back to a convectively active tropical wave that emerged off the coast of west Africa on 18 August. The wave continued to produce a large area of disorganized showers and thunderstorms during the next several days while it traveled quickly across the eastern and central tropical Atlantic. The tropical wave came close to becoming a tropical cyclone in the central tropical Atlantic before weakening as it moved across the Windward Islands late on 24 August. Convection became more intermittent as the wave traveled through the Caribbean Sea over the next few days, then became more robust as it crossed Central America beginning late on 26 August. The wave emerged over the eastern Pacific Ocean on 28 August, and convection gradually became better organized during the next several days while the system moved west-northwestward well to the south of Guatemala and southern Mexico. The thunderstorm activity became sufficiently organized by 0000 UTC 2 September for the system to be designated a tropical depression when it was located about 120 n mi south of Manzanillo, Mexico, and it became a tropical storm 6 h later. The “best track” chart of Lorena’s path is given in Fig. 1, with the wind and pressure histories shown in Figs. 2 and 3, respectively. The best track positions and intensities are listed in Table 11. During the next couple of days, Lorena tracked northwestward over very warm waters near 30ºC, within a moist mid-level environment, while influenced by light vertical wind shear. Steady-to-rapid intensification ensued, with the cyclone becoming a category 1 hurricane (on the Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale) 24 h after becoming a tropical storm at 0600 UTC 3 September about 85 n mi southwest of Cabo San Lucas, Mexico. The cyclone remained a hurricane for about a day and reached its estimated peak intensity of 75 kt from 1800 UTC 3 September–0000 UTC 4 September. The evolution of a well-defined eyewall feature was captured by radar imagery from Los Cabos, Mexico, on 3 September as shown in Fig. 4. Environmental conditions became hostile rather abruptly on 4 September, as Lorena became influenced by increasingly strong southwesterly vertical wind shear while moving over progressively cooler waters below 26°C. These conditions resulted in rapid weakening of the cyclone, with Lorena losing deep convection shortly after 1800 UTC 4 September and becoming a post-tropical cyclone by 0000 UTC 5 September. The post-tropical cyclone meandered a couple of hundred n mi offshore of the west coast of Baja California for the next day or so, then tracked slowly northwestward and eventually opened into a trough by 1200 UTC 7 September.

## METEOROLOGICAL STATISTICS

Observations in Lorena (Figs. 2 and 3) include subjective satellite-based Dvorak technique intensity estimates from the Tropical Analysis and Forecast Branch (TAFB) and the Satellite Analysis Branch (SAB), objective Advanced Dvorak Technique (ADT) estimates and Satellite Consensus (SATCON) estimates from the Cooperative Institute for Meteorological Satellite Studies/University of Wisconsin-Madison. Observations also include flight-level and dropwindsonde observations from two flights of the 53rd Weather Reconnaissance Squadron of the U.S. Air Force Reserve Command (AFRES) (Fig. 5). Data and imagery from NOAA polar-orbiting satellites including the Advanced Microwave Sounding Unit (AMSU), the NASA Global Precipitation Mission (GPM), the European Space Agency’s Advanced Scatterometer (ASCAT), the Defense Meteorological Satellite Program (DMSP) satellites, and the Time-Resolved Observations of Precipitation structure and storm Intensity with a Constellation of Smallsats (TROPICS) satellites, among others, were also useful in constructing the best track of Lorena. The container ship OOCL Egypt (call sign VRLZ3) reported a sustained tropical-storm-force wind of 37 kt in association with Lorena at 1200 UTC 2 September while located about 85 n mi west of Manzanillo, Mexico. There were no land-based reports of sustained tropical-storm-force winds associated with Lorena. Tropical-storm-force gusts were reported across the southern portion of Baja California Sur, however, with a 53-kt wind gust measured in Cabo San Lucas and a 44-kt gust measured in Sierra La Laguna (Table 2).

Winds and Pressure Lorena’s estimated peak intensity of 75 kt from 1800 UTC 3 September to 0000 UTC 4 September is based on a blend of aircraft data and TAFB subjective Dvorak intensity estimates. The two reconnaissance missions by the AFRES into Lorena provided valuable data. The first mission sampled peak flight-level winds of 79 kt, 6 n mi to the northeast of the flight-level center at 1700 UTC 3 September. Using standard reduction factors from flight-level (700 mb), the surface winds were estimated to be around 71 kt. During this same mission, a dropsonde measured a 70-kt surface wind in the northeast eyewall. The WL150 and MBL winds from this dropsonde both suggested surface winds of 64 and 63 kt respectively. These data support a peak intensity of 75 kt when accounting for undersampling, and are further supported by the TAFB subjective Dvorak intensity estimates of T4.5/77 kt at 1800 UTC 3 September and 0000 UTC 4 September.

Lorena’s minimum pressure of 981 mb was based on the Knaff-Zehr-Courtney pressure-wind relationship. This pressure occurred at 0000 UTC 4 September, late in the afternoon when the diurnal pressure cycle is at its lowest point. The first AFRES mission into Lorena measured a minimum central pressure of 985 mb with 7 kt of wind at the surface (10 m) at 1759 UTC 3 September, which supports a minimum central pressure of 984 mb at that time.

Rainfall and Flooding Heavy rainfall affected portions of southern Baja California Sur (Fig. 6) as Lorena passed about 100 n mi to the west. The highest reported storm-total rainfall was 12.28 inches (312.0 mm) at Santa Anita, followed by 10.43 inches (265.0 mm) at Yeneka and 10.24 inches (260.0 mm) at Boca del Salado. Additional notable totals included 8.98 inches (228.0 mm) at San José del Cabo and 7.86 inches (199.6 mm) at El Sauzal (Table 2). These very heavy rains produced flooding across portions of the municipality of Los Cabos and surrounding areas.

## CASUALTY AND DAMAGE STATISTICS

No fatalities or injuries can confidently be attributed to Lorena. There were several media reports indicating Lorena was responsible for several fatalities in the Mexican states of Nayarit and Sinaloa. However, after reviewing the articles and cross-referencing satellite imagery for the dates and times when the fatalities occurred, it was determined that these deaths were neither directly or indirectly related to Lorena. In Baja California Sur, impacts were concentrated in the municipalities of Los Cabos, La Paz, Mulegé, Loreto, and Comondú. Heavy rainfall and flooding resulted in water inundation of at least eight homes, while three additional residences were reported to have been swept away by floodwaters. Civil protection authorities reported approximately 50 vehicles stranded due to flooded or damaged roadways. Four landslides were also documented, and there was a minor disruption to electrical service associated with damage to a single power pole. Roadways across Los Cabos were engulfed in thick mud and debris due to the flooding, necessitating a substantial response and recovery effort. A total of 119 people sought refuge in four temporary shelters during the event. Numerous federal, state, and municipal agencies participated in preparedness and emergency response activities.

## FORECAST AND WARNING CRITIQUE

The genesis of Lorena was well anticipated. The tropical wave from which Lorena developed was first highlighted in the Tropical Weather Outlook (TWO) 156 h prior to formation (Table 3). The 7-day genesis probability was raised to the medium category 114 h before formation and to the high category 72 h in advance. Two-day genesis forecasts were also excellent. Low probabilities were introduced 78 h prior to formation, medium probabilities 48 h in advance, though high probabilities were introduced only 6 h before development occurred. Forecasts of Lorena’s genesis location were highly accurate, with the system forming within each of the genesis forecast areas depicted in NHC’s Graphical TWO, resulting in a 100% hit rate (Fig. 7). A verification of NHC official track forecasts for Lorena is given in Table 4a. Official track forecast errors were higher than the mean official errors for the previous 5-yr period at all forecast intervals. This is likely attributable to a bias in some of the model guidance keeping Lorena a deeper cyclone longer and steering the system into Baja California, while in actuality Lorena weakened much faster than anticipated and remained well offshore of Baja California steered by the low-level flow. A homogeneous comparison of the official track errors with selected guidance models is given in Table 4b. The official track forecasts outperformed all of the single-model aids at all forecast intervals, with the exception of EMXI and HWFI which performed better than the official forecast for a couple of periods. The consensus models generally outperformed the official track forecasts. The Google DeepMind Ensemble Mean (GDMI) was particularly skillful, outperforming the official forecasts at all forecast intervals, and also correctly predicted the cyclone would stall off the coast of Baja California. The Hurricane Forecast Improvement Program (HFIP) Corrected Consensus Approach (HCCA), Florida State Super Ensemble (FSSE), and GFEX models also performed better than the official forecasts at most time intervals. A verification of NHC official intensity forecasts for Lorena is given in Table 5a. Official intensity forecast errors were higher than the mean official errors for the previous 5-yr period at all forecast intervals, except for the 60-h interval. The larger than normal forecast errors are likely attributable to a bias in some of the models keeping the system stronger for a longer period than what actually occurred. A homogeneous comparison of the official intensity errors with selected guidance models is given in Table 5b. The official intensity forecasts were outperformed by a considerable number of guidance models, with the HCCA and FSSE models performing better than the official forecast at all forecast intervals, and the ICON, IVCN, and IVDR models outperforming the official forecast at most time intervals.
