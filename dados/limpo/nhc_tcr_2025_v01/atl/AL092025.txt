## SYNOPTIC HISTORY

Imelda originated from a tropical wave that moved off the west coast of Africa on 16 September. The wave produced some disorganized showers and thunderstorms as it moved westward at about 15 kt and passed over the Cabo Verde Islands on 17 September. Convective activity diminished during the next several days as the wave moved through a drier mid-level environment over the eastern and central tropical Atlantic. Showers and thunderstorms then gradually increased, but remained disorganized, on 21–22 September as the wave approached the Leeward Islands. Moving west-northwestward, the convectively active wave crossed the Leeward Islands on 23 September and then brought gusty winds and heavy rainfall to Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands while crossing the region on 24 and early 25 September. Showers and thunderstorms began showing increased signs of organization later on 25 and 26 September while the disturbance passed over Hispaniola and eastern Cuba. However, satellite images and scatterometer data indicated the tropical wave still lacked a well-defined, closed circulation center. Given the high likelihood of development and the threat of tropical storm conditions to land areas, the NHC initiated advisories on Potential Tropical Cyclone Nine at 2100 UTC 26 September. At that time, the broad low-level vorticity maximum was located about 50 n mi north-northwest of the eastern tip of Cuba. The “best track” is given in Fig. 1, with the wind and pressure histories shown in Figs. 2 and 3, respectively. The best track positions and intensities are listed in Table 11. The disturbance moved slowly northwestward on 26–27 September along the southwestern extent of a subtropical ridge over the western Atlantic. During this time, the convection did not become significantly better organized, and dropsonde data from Hurricane Hunter aircraft indicate the system remained an open surface trough between eastern Cuba and the southeastern Bahamas. The low-level circulation became better defined in satellite images late on 27 September, and convection was sufficiently organized to mark the formation of a tropical depression by 0000 UTC 28 September, when it was centered about 240 n mi south of Great Abaco Island in the northwestern Bahamas. Convective banding became more pronounced over the eastern side of the circulation later that day, and the depression strengthened into Tropical Storm Imelda by 1800 UTC 28 September when it was located about 155 n mi south of Great Abaco. Although the vortex was tilted with height due to moderate southerly shear (Fig. 4), Imelda gradually strengthened over 30°C waters near the Bahamas while it moved northward along the western side of the subtropical ridge. Imelda made landfall as a 45-kt tropical storm on Great Abaco around 1200 UTC 29 September. September as continued shear and intrusions of dry air disrupted Imelda’s convective organization and briefly left the low-level center exposed (Fig. 6, left). Meanwhile, the northward progress of Imelda slowed as nearby Hurricane Humberto2, located a little more than 400 n mi to the east of Imelda, weakened the western Atlantic subtropical ridge. In response, Imelda turned sharply east-northeastward within the southwesterly flow ahead of an approaching mid-latitude trough over the eastern United States. Intensification resumed later on 30 September as new inner core convection consolidated around the center of Imelda, and it became a hurricane by 1200 UTC that day about 150 n mi north of Great Abaco. Enhanced upper-level divergence associated with the upper trough and warm sea-surface temperatures supported continued strengthening of the cyclone on 1 October as Imelda accelerated east-northeastward toward Bermuda. A ragged eye became apparent in satellite images (Fig. 6, right), and Imelda reached a peak intensity of 80 kt by 1200 UTC 1 October, when it was centered about 355 n mi west-southwest of Bermuda. Extratropical transition began as Imelda continued to interact with the upper trough on its approach to Bermuda. Intrusions of dry air started to erode the inner core, and convection became displaced to the north of the cyclone center. In addition, the wind field grew in size and became asymmetric, with a narrow band of strong winds wrapping around the northern and western sides of the circulation as Imelda began to interact with a nearby front. The center of Imelda passed about 5–10 n mi south of Bermuda early on 2 October, with Imelda making its closest approach to the island around 0300 UTC as an 80-kt hurricane (Fig. 7). Synthetic aperture radar data from later that morning (Fig. 8) indicate Imelda became a frontal low and completed its extratropical transition by 1200 UTC 2 October, while centered about 200 n mi east-northeast of Bermuda. The powerful cyclone continued east-northeastward through early 3 October, then turned northward and briefly strengthened into a hurricane-force extratropical low by 0000 UTC 4 October. Then, the system accelerated northeastward over the northern Atlantic and gradually weakened over the next couple of days before being absorbed by another frontal system by 0600 UTC 6 October.

## METEOROLOGICAL STATISTICS

Observations in Imelda (Figs. 2 and 3) include subjective satellite-based Dvorak technique intensity estimates from the Tropical Analysis and Forecast Branch (TAFB) and the Satellite Analysis Branch (SAB), objective Advanced Dvorak Technique (ADT) estimates and Satellite Consensus (SATCON) estimates from the Cooperative Institute for Meteorological Satellite Studies/University of Wisconsin-Madison. Data and imagery from NOAA polar-orbiting satellites including the Advanced Microwave Sounding Unit (AMSU), the NASA Global Precipitation Mission (GPM), the European Space Agency’s Advanced Scatterometer (ASCAT), the Defense Meteorological Satellite Program (DMSP) satellites, and the Time-Resolved Observations of Precipitation structure and storm Intensity with a Constellation of Smallsats (TROPICS) satellites, among others, were also useful in constructing the best track of Imelda. 2 Hagen, Andrew B. “Tropical Cyclone Report: Hurricane Humberto.” National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration / National Weather Service / National Hurricane Center, 11 Dec. 2025, https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/data/tcr/AL082025_Humberto.pdf Aircraft observations include flight-level and dropwindsonde data from 11 flights of the 53rd Weather Reconnaissance Squadron of the U.S. Air Force Reserve Command and 16 flights of the NOAA Hurricane Hunters (Fig. 9). Of these 16 NOAA flights, 8 were synoptic surveillance missions by the Gulfstream-IV jet. Several of these missions successfully performed circumnavigations of two systems (93L/Humberto and 94L/Imelda) on the same flight, which was the first time that this strategy has been employed. In total, the Air Force Reserve and NOAA Hurricane Hunters provided 42 center fixes for Imelda from 27 September–1 October. Winds and Pressure The estimated peak intensity of Imelda (80 kt) on 1 October is based on a blend of aircraft data and satellite intensity estimates. An Air Force Reserve Hurricane Hunter flight on 1 October measured peak 700-mb flight-level winds of 98 kt at 2140 UTC, which would typically correspond to a surface intensity of 88 kt using a standard 90% adjustment factor. However, Imelda was in the early stages of extratropical transition at this time, and layer-averaged dropsonde winds from that day suggest a standard reduction was likely not representative. A 1458 UTC eyewall dropsonde recorded mean boundary layer (0-500 m) and WL150 (0-150 m layer average) winds of 90 kt, which both reduce to an intensity of around 75 kt. Dropsonde data from the following mission supported even weaker surface-adjusted winds. Subjective Dvorak estimates from TAFB and SAB, as well as objective ADT and AiDT estimates from UW-CIMSS, peaked at 77 kt on 1 October. A blend of the various datasets supports a peak intensity of 80 kt. The estimated minimum central pressure of 966 mb at 1800 UTC 1 October is based on aircraft data. A 1453 UTC center dropsonde from the Air Force Reserve Hurricane Hunters recorded a surface pressure of 967 mb with 16-kt surface winds, and a 1634 UTC center dropsonde measured a surface pressure of 968 mb with 20-kt surface winds. Some of the northwestern and central Bahamas experienced tropical storm conditions on 28–29 September as Imelda moved over the region. A WeatherSTEM station on Blue Lagoon Island near Nassau measured a sustained wind of 36 kt and a gust of 41 kt at 1808 UTC 28 September. The next day, an unofficial weather station on Hog Cay in the northwestern Bahamas reported a sustained wind of 50 kt and a gust of 72 kt as the center of Imelda passed nearby. A few coastal locations in east-central Florida experienced tropical-storm-force gusts on 29–30 September as the outer western portion of Imelda’s circulation grazed the coastline. A gust of 37 kt was reported at Parrish Park in Titusville, and a 35-kt gust was measured in New Smyrna Beach. Imelda brought hurricane conditions to Bermuda early on 2 October as the center of the storm passed just south of the island. An elevated weather station at the National Museum of Bermuda (45 m above sea level) measured a sustained wind of 67 kt at 0359 UTC and a gust of 86 kt. Elsewhere, a sustained wind of 41 kt and a gust of 57 kt were reported at L.F. Wade International Airport (TXKF) on the northeastern part of the island. The minimum pressure recorded on the island was 974.7 mb at the National Museum of Bermuda. Rainfall and Flooding Greater Antilles The tropical wave that spawned Imelda produced heavy rainfall across much of the Greater Antilles as it moved across the region. The southern part of Puerto Rico generally received 2–4 inches of rainfall from 24–26 September, with a storm total maximum of 5.69 inches at a CoCoRaHS station near Cabo Rojo (PR-CR-13). These heavy rains caused flash flooding and some landslides, especially across southern portions of the island. Flooding was also reported along the Blanco, Cotui, and Viejo Rivers. Significant flash and river flooding occurred in the Dominican Republic (Fig. 10) as 4–8 inches (~100–200 mm) of rain with locally higher amounts fell across southern and eastern portions of the country from 23–27 September, with a storm total maximum of 21.13 inches (536.6 mm) in Oviedo. Media reports indicate heavy rainfall and flooding also occurred in Haiti. An extended period of heavy rainfall occurred in eastern Cuba from 26–30 September, with numerous stations in Guantánamo and Santiago de Cuba receiving more than 12 inches (~ 300 mm) of rain from Imelda and its precursor disturbance. A storm total maximum rainfall of 26.87 inches (682.4 mm) was reported at Hatibonico, and 22.00 inches (559.0 mm) fell in Aserradero. These heavy rains triggered widespread flash flooding, landslides, and river flooding that required some water rescues. ## CASUALTY AND DAMAGE STATISTICS

Imelda is responsible for 2 direct fatalities4 in the Santiago de Cuba province of eastern Cuba due to freshwater flooding, including a 60-year-old man who died in a landslide following heavy rainfall. In Puerto Rico, a 36-year-old man drowned when his car was swept away by floodwaters after heavy rainfall caused the Yauco River to overflow its banks on 24 September5. This fatality is attributed to the parent tropical wave from which Imelda formed. There are no official damage estimates available at the time of this report. Southeastern United States Large swells from both Imelda and distant Hurricane Humberto caused an extended period of rough surf and damaging waves along the southeastern U.S. coast. In Florida, some minor to moderate beach erosion was noted by officials in Brevard and Volusia Counties. These would include those persons who drowned in storm surge, rough seas, rip currents, and freshwater floods. Direct deaths also include casualties resulting from lightning and wind-related events (e.g., collapsing structures). Deaths occurring from such factors as heart attacks, house fires, electrocutions from downed power lines, vehicle accidents on wet roads, etc., are considered “indirect” deaths.

Greater Antilles Imelda and its predecessor tropical wave produced damaging floods across portions of Puerto Rico, the Dominican Republic, Haiti, and Cuba7. In Puerto Rico, several landslides blocked roads, and other roads were inundated by swollen rivers. Some downed trees and utility poles were also reported. In the Dominican Republic, 2 homes were destroyed and almost 1,000 homes were damaged by flooding. River flooding temporarily cut off some communities, and gusty winds over the saturated ground led to some downed trees that blocked roads and damaged a couple of homes. Elsewhere, flooding affected 35 communities in Haiti and caused significant crop damage. In eastern Cuba, flooding and landslides cut off 17 communities in the Santiago de Cuba province affecting more than 24,000 people. Over 18,000 people were evacuated in the Guantánamo province, and damaged roads and bridges were reported where numerous landslides and extensive flash flooding occurred. The Bahamas No major structural damage was reported in the Bahamas. Significant flooding occurred in parts of Nassau and surrounding communities on the island of New Providence, including Carmichael, Pinewood Gardens, Gamble Heights, Fox Hill, Bel Air Estates, and West Grove8. Numerous homes and buildings were inundated by floodwaters, including about 100 homes in Carmichael. Some residents and vehicles were stranded by floodwaters, and several water rescues were performed. More than a dozen schools in New Providence, Grand Bahama, and Abaco were closed due to flooding concerns. Gusty winds downed some power lines, with widespread power outages reported in Abaco and more than 1,500 customers affected in New Providence.9 Bermuda No significant damage or casualties occurred in Bermuda. Strong winds downed some trees and power lines across the island, and about 18,000 customers lost power at the height of the event. In Pembroke Parish, the roof of a former hotel was ripped off, and an exterior wall collapsed, which damaged a nearby vehicle (Fig. 11).10

## FORECAST AND WARNING CRITIQUE

Genesis The genesis of Imelda was anticipated well in advance, although the system formed much later than originally forecast (Table 4). The wave from which Imelda developed was originally introduced in the 7-day Tropical Weather Outlook (TWO) shortly after it emerged off the west coast of Africa, 282 h (11.75 days) prior to genesis. A low 2-day chance of formation 10 https://www.royalgazette.com/weather/news/article/20251003/storm-takes-out-wall-at-former-hotel/ 9 https://www.tribune242.com/news/2025/sep/30/imelda-leaves-behind-flooding-in-its-wake/ 8 https://www.thenassauguardian.com/news/flooded-out/article_5c819cc7-d3fb-44d3-a6ea-a39183462553.html 7 https://apnews.com/article/hurricane-imelda-humberto-bermuda-c6760b885b541c2154dcb1b5b9bcf163 was introduced 264 h (11 days) prior to formation. However, the wave was embedded in a dry mid-level environment and failed to develop during the following couple of days, and the system was dropped from the TWO on 18 September. Several days later, the tropical wave was reintroduced into the 7-day and 2-day TWO with a low chance of formation 150 h (6.25 days) prior to genesis. The 7-day probabilities were raised to the medium and high categories 132 h and 96 h before formation, respectively. For the 2-day TWO, the system reached the medium category 72 h and the high category 60 h before genesis occurred. The genesis forecasts for Imelda were challenging due to inconsistent global model and ensemble signals as to when development might occur, likely related to the hostile environmental conditions that the wave encountered within the Main Development Region during the climatological peak of the season. A verification of NHC Graphical TWO (GTWO) genesis forecasts for Imelda is shown in Figure 12. The struggles to pinpoint the timing of formation also made it difficult to predict exactly where Imelda would form, as shown by the 43% overall GTWO hit rate. Early NHC genesis forecasts indicated a low chance of formation over the eastern and central tropical Atlantic, but this did not occur due to the marginal environmental conditions. After the wave was reintroduced into the GTWO, the NHC genesis forecasts generally highlighted the southwestern Atlantic Ocean for formation, but most of the medium probability areas were east of where Imelda ultimately formed. The high category areas all correctly captured the location of genesis. Track A verification of NHC official track forecasts (OFCL) for Imelda is given in Table 5a. Official track forecast errors were comparable to or lower than the mean official errors for the previous 5-yr period from 12–60 h, and greater than the mean errors at 72 h. For the two verifying 96-h forecasts, OFCL errors were over 3.5 times larger than the 5-yr means. A homogeneous comparison of the official track errors with selected guidance models is given in Table 5b. The OFCL forecasts outperformed many of the individual models and consensus aids from 12–72 h, although OFCL was generally bested by the UKMET (UKMI) and Google DeepMind ensemble (GDMI) during this period. At 96 h, OFCL had larger errors than most of the guidance, albeit for only 2 verifying forecasts. The close proximity of Humberto and Imelda and the potential for binary interaction made early track forecasts very challenging. In particular, there was a bifurcation in the long-range track guidance available when advisories were initiated (Fig. 13). While some models (GFS, HAFS-A/B, ECMWF) showed a close approach to or even a landfall along the southeastern U.S. coast, others (COAMPS-TC and UKMET) favored a sharp eastward turn farther offshore. These diverging track solutions were also noted in the various global ensembles (not shown). Humberto and pre-Imelda were at roughly the same latitude on 26–27 September, but Imelda developed more gradually and moved northward more slowly than Humberto on 28–30 September (Fig. 14). As a result, Humberto eroded the steering ridge over the western Atlantic, which kept Imelda farther offshore than originally forecast. While the official NHC forecast never explicitly predicted landfall, early NHC forecasts showed a much closer approach to land than what ultimately occurred before trending eastward in response to large shifts in the track guidance (Fig. 15). Once the advancing upper-level trough over the eastern U.S. became the predominant steering feature (due to the slower storm motion of Imelda), the tropical cyclone turned sharply east-northeastward away from the U.S. and accelerated toward Bermuda. Notably, the UKMET was among the first dynamical models to show Imelda making this sharp eastward turn away from the U.S. coastline (Fig. 13). The NHC track forecasts consistently showed Imelda making a close approach to Bermuda for several days prior to the hurricane impacting the island (Fig. 16). The NHC intensity forecasts for Imelda were very good, with OFCL errors comparable to the 5-yr mean at 12 h and lower than the 5-yr means at 24–96 h. Figure 17 shows all of the NHC intensity forecasts for Imelda compared to the best track. The official forecasts generally captured the periods of strengthening and weakening reasonably well, although Imelda did not intensify as quickly as initially predicted. The NHC intensity forecasts beat almost all of the individual models between 24–96 h, and OFCL bested HCCA, FSSE, and GDMI at a majority of verified forecast times. The NHC initiated potential tropical cyclone advisories at 2100 UTC 26 September on the precursor disturbance, which enabled the Bahamas to issue Tropical Storm Watches and Warnings before genesis occurred.

For the United States, a Tropical Storm Watch was issued for the east coast of Florida from the Palm Beach/Martin County Line to the Flagler/Volusia County Line at 1500 UTC 27 September. The watch was never upgraded to a warning since subsequent track forecasts shifted farther offshore. While no sustained winds of tropical storm force occurred along the coast, a few locations within the watch area did report tropical-storm-force gusts on 29–30 September.

## IMPACT-BASED DECISION SUPPORT SERVICES & PUBLIC

## COMMUNICATION

The NHC began formal communication with emergency managers on 25 September before the disturbance that became Imelda reached the Bahamas. Thirteen decision support briefings were provided to emergency managers and coordinated through the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) Hurricane Liaison Team embedded at the NHC. These briefings included video-teleconferences with FEMA HQ, FEMA Region 4, the states of South Carolina and North Carolina, and the state and counties of Florida. Briefing support continued through 29 September, after which it became clear that Imelda would not threaten the United States. In addition, the NHC/TAFB provided seven briefings to the U.S. Coast Guard Southeast District from 27–30 September in support of their life-saving mission.
