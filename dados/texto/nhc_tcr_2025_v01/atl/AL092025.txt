SYNOPTIC HISTORY 
​
​
Imelda originated from a tropical wave that moved off the west coast of Africa on 16 
September. The wave produced some disorganized showers and thunderstorms as it moved 
westward at about 15 kt and passed over the Cabo Verde Islands on 17 September. Convective 
activity diminished during the next several days as the wave moved through a drier mid-level 
environment over the eastern and central tropical Atlantic. Showers and thunderstorms then 
gradually increased, but remained disorganized, on 21–22 September as the wave approached 
the Leeward Islands. Moving west-northwestward, the convectively active wave crossed the 
Leeward Islands on 23 September and then brought gusty winds and heavy rainfall to Puerto 
Rico and the Virgin Islands while crossing the region on 24 and early 25 September. Showers 
and thunderstorms began showing increased signs of organization later on 25 and 26 
September while the disturbance passed over Hispaniola and eastern Cuba. However, satellite 
images and scatterometer data indicated the tropical wave still lacked a well-defined, closed 
circulation center. Given the high likelihood of development and the threat of tropical storm 
conditions to land areas, the NHC initiated advisories on Potential Tropical Cyclone Nine at 
2100 UTC 26 September. At that time, the broad low-level vorticity maximum was located about 
50 n mi north-northwest of the eastern tip of Cuba. The “best track” is given in Fig. 1, with the 
wind and pressure histories shown in Figs. 2 and 3, respectively. The best track positions and 
intensities are listed in Table 11. 
The disturbance moved slowly northwestward on 26–27 September along the 
southwestern extent of a subtropical ridge over the western Atlantic. During this time, the 
convection did not become significantly better organized, and dropsonde data from Hurricane 
Hunter aircraft indicate the system remained an open surface trough between eastern Cuba and 
the southeastern Bahamas. The low-level circulation became better defined in satellite images 
late on 27 September, and convection was sufficiently organized to mark the formation of a 
tropical depression by 0000 UTC 28 September, when it was centered about 240 n mi south of 
Great Abaco Island in the northwestern Bahamas. Convective banding became more 
pronounced over the eastern side of the circulation later that day, and the depression 
strengthened into Tropical Storm Imelda by 1800 UTC 28 September when it was located about 
155 n mi south of Great Abaco. Although the vortex was tilted with height due to moderate 
southerly shear (Fig. 4), Imelda gradually strengthened over 30°C waters near the Bahamas 
while it moved northward along the western side of the subtropical ridge. Imelda made landfall 
as a 45-kt tropical storm on Great Abaco around 1200 UTC 29 September. 
The strengthening of Imelda continued later on 29 September while the system moved 
over 28–29°C waters north of the Bahamas (Fig. 5), but then the intensity leveled off early on 30

became a hurricane by 1200 UTC that day about 150 n mi north of Great Abaco. Enhanced 
upper-level divergence associated with the upper trough and warm sea-surface temperatures 
supported continued strengthening of the cyclone on 1 October as Imelda accelerated 
east-northeastward toward Bermuda. A ragged eye became apparent in satellite images (Fig. 6, 
right), and Imelda reached a peak intensity of 80 kt by 1200 UTC 1 October, when it was 
centered about 355 n mi west-southwest of Bermuda. 
Extratropical transition began as Imelda continued to interact with the upper trough on its 
approach to Bermuda. Intrusions of dry air started to erode the inner core, and convection 
became displaced to the north of the cyclone center. In addition, the wind field grew in size and 
became asymmetric, with a narrow band of strong winds wrapping around the northern and 
western sides of the circulation as Imelda began to interact with a nearby front. The center of 
Imelda passed about 5–10 n mi south of Bermuda early on 2 October, with Imelda making its 
closest approach to the island around 0300 UTC as an 80-kt hurricane (Fig. 7). Synthetic 
aperture radar data from later that morning (Fig. 8) indicate Imelda became a frontal low and 
completed its extratropical transition by 1200 UTC 2 October, while centered about 200 n mi 
east-northeast of Bermuda. The powerful cyclone continued east-northeastward through early 3 
October, then turned northward and briefly strengthened into a hurricane-force extratropical low 
by 0000 UTC 4 October. Then, the system accelerated northeastward over the northern Atlantic 
and gradually weakened over the next couple of days before being absorbed by another frontal 
system by 0600 UTC 6 October.

Winds and Pressure 
The estimated peak intensity of Imelda (80 kt) on 1 October is based on a blend of 
aircraft data and satellite intensity estimates. An Air Force Reserve Hurricane Hunter flight on 1 
October measured peak 700-mb flight-level winds of 98 kt at 2140 UTC, which would typically 
correspond to a surface intensity of 88 kt using a standard 90% adjustment factor. However, 
Imelda was in the early stages of extratropical transition at this time, and layer-averaged 
dropsonde winds from that day suggest a standard reduction was likely not representative. A 
1458 UTC eyewall dropsonde recorded mean boundary layer (0-500 m) and WL150 (0-150 m 
layer average) winds of 90 kt, which both reduce to an intensity of around 75 kt. Dropsonde data 
from the following mission supported even weaker surface-adjusted winds. Subjective Dvorak 
estimates from TAFB and SAB, as well as objective ADT and AiDT estimates from UW-CIMSS, 
peaked at 77 kt on 1 October. A blend of the various datasets supports a peak intensity of 80 kt. 
The estimated minimum central pressure of 966 mb at 1800 UTC 1 October is based on 
aircraft data. A 1453 UTC center dropsonde from the Air Force Reserve Hurricane Hunters 
recorded a surface pressure of 967 mb with 16-kt surface winds, and a 1634 UTC center 
dropsonde measured a surface pressure of 968 mb with 20-kt surface winds. 
Some of the northwestern and central Bahamas experienced tropical storm conditions 
on 28–29 September as Imelda moved over the region. A WeatherSTEM station on Blue 
Lagoon Island near Nassau measured a sustained wind of 36 kt and a gust of 41 kt at 1808 
UTC 28 September. The next day, an unofficial weather station on Hog Cay in the northwestern 
Bahamas reported a sustained wind of 50 kt and a gust of 72 kt as the center of Imelda passed 
nearby. 
A few coastal locations in east-central Florida experienced tropical-storm-force gusts on 
29–30 September as the outer western portion of Imelda’s circulation grazed the coastline. A 
gust of 37 kt was reported at Parrish Park in Titusville, and a 35-kt gust was measured in New 
Smyrna Beach. 
Imelda brought hurricane conditions to Bermuda early on 2 October as the center of the 
storm passed just south of the island. An elevated weather station at the National Museum of 
Bermuda (45 m above sea level) measured a sustained wind of 67 kt at 0359 UTC and a gust of 
86 kt. Elsewhere, a sustained wind of 41 kt and a gust of 57 kt were reported at L.F. Wade

Storm Surge3 
For Bermuda, the highest measured storm surge reached 2.25 feet above normal tide 
levels along the north coast of St. George’s Island on 2 October. This observed peak occurred 
at low tide, yielding only minor coastal inundation (i.e., 0.56 feet above Mean Higher High Water 
[MHHW]). However, elevated water levels persisted into the following high tide cycle, resulting in 
a peak storm tide (combination of storm surge and astronomical tide) of 1.09 ft above MHHW. It 
should be noted that peak water level observations are not available along the south coast of 
Bermuda, where the strongest onshore winds are likely to have occurred. 
Rainfall and Flooding 
Greater Antilles 
The tropical wave that spawned Imelda produced heavy rainfall across much of the 
Greater Antilles as it moved across the region. The southern part of Puerto Rico generally 
received 2–4 inches of rainfall from 24–26 September, with a storm total maximum of 5.69 
inches at a CoCoRaHS station near Cabo Rojo (PR-CR-13). These heavy rains caused flash 
flooding and some landslides, especially across southern portions of the island. Flooding was 
also reported along the Blanco, Cotui, and Viejo Rivers. Significant flash and river flooding 
occurred in the Dominican Republic (Fig. 10) as 4–8 inches (~100–200 mm) of rain with locally 
higher amounts fell across southern and eastern portions of the country from 23–27 September, 
with a storm total maximum of 21.13 inches (536.6 mm) in Oviedo. Media reports indicate heavy 
rainfall and flooding also occurred in Haiti. 
An extended period of heavy rainfall occurred in eastern Cuba from 26–30 September, 
with numerous stations in Guantánamo and Santiago de Cuba receiving more than 12 inches (~ 
300 mm) of rain from Imelda and its precursor disturbance. A storm total maximum rainfall of 
26.87 inches (682.4 mm) was reported at Hatibonico, and 22.00 inches (559.0 mm) fell in 
Aserradero. These heavy rains triggered widespread flash flooding, landslides, and river 
flooding that required some water rescues.

Outer rainbands from Imelda produced 1–3 inches of rain along portions of the east 
coast of Florida, with a storm total maximum of 4.35 inches measured at a CoCoRaHS station 
near Ormond Beach (FL-VL-104). The interaction of peripheral moisture from Imelda with a

Bahamas 
Imelda produced flooding rains across the northwestern and central Bahamas from 
27–30 September. The heaviest rainfall occurred on the island of New Providence, with a storm 
total maximum of 14.87 inches of rain measured at the Lynden Pindling International Airport 
(MYNN) near Nassau. Other locations on that island received 6–10 inches of rainfall during the 
3-day period. In Great Abaco, 7.76 inches of rain was reported at a CoCoRaHS station 
(BHS-CO-4) near Cherokee. Elsewhere, storm total rainfall amounts of 3–6 inches were 
reported on other islands including San Salvador, Great Harbour Cay, and Eleuthera. Media 
reports indicate flooding occurred on the islands of New Providence, Abaco, and San Salvador. 
Bermuda 
Imelda was undergoing extratropical transition as it passed near Bermuda, and the 
storm’s fast forward speed limited the duration of rainfall that occurred on the island. The L.F. 
Wade International Airport (TXKF) reported 1.30 inches of rain. No significant flooding was 
reported. 
 
CASUALTY AND DAMAGE STATISTICS​
 
Imelda is responsible for 2 direct fatalities4 in the Santiago de Cuba province of eastern 
Cuba due to freshwater flooding, including a 60-year-old man who died in a landslide following 
heavy rainfall. In Puerto Rico, a 36-year-old man drowned when his car was swept away by 
floodwaters after heavy rainfall caused the Yauco River to overflow its banks on 24 September5. 
This fatality is attributed to the parent tropical wave from which Imelda formed. There are no 
official damage estimates available at the time of this report. 
Southeastern United States 
​
Large swells from both Imelda and distant Hurricane Humberto caused an extended 
period of rough surf and damaging waves along the southeastern U.S. coast. In Florida, some 
minor to moderate beach erosion was noted by officials in Brevard and Volusia Counties. 
Farther north in the Outer Banks of North Carolina, more significant beach erosion caused by 
battering waves led to 6 unoccupied homes in Buxton collapsing into the ocean.6

Greater Antilles 
​
Imelda and its predecessor tropical wave produced damaging floods across portions of 
Puerto Rico, the Dominican Republic, Haiti, and Cuba7. In Puerto Rico, several landslides 
blocked roads, and other roads were inundated by swollen rivers. Some downed trees and utility 
poles were also reported. In the Dominican Republic, 2 homes were destroyed and almost 
1,000 homes were damaged by flooding. River flooding temporarily cut off some communities, 
and gusty winds over the saturated ground led to some downed trees that blocked roads and 
damaged a couple of homes. Elsewhere, flooding affected 35 communities in Haiti and caused 
significant crop damage. In eastern Cuba, flooding and landslides cut off 17 communities in the 
Santiago de Cuba province affecting more than 24,000 people. Over 18,000 people were 
evacuated in the Guantánamo province, and damaged roads and bridges were reported where 
numerous landslides and extensive flash flooding occurred. 
The Bahamas 
​
No major structural damage was reported in the Bahamas. Significant flooding occurred 
in parts of Nassau and surrounding communities on the island of New Providence, including 
Carmichael, Pinewood Gardens, Gamble Heights, Fox Hill, Bel Air Estates, and West Grove8. 
Numerous homes and buildings were inundated by floodwaters, including about 100 homes in 
Carmichael. Some residents and vehicles were stranded by floodwaters, and several water 
rescues were performed. More than a dozen schools in New Providence, Grand Bahama, and 
Abaco were closed due to flooding concerns. Gusty winds downed some power lines, with 
widespread power outages reported in Abaco and more than 1,500 customers affected in New 
Providence.9 
Bermuda 
​
No significant damage or casualties occurred in Bermuda. Strong winds downed some 
trees and power lines across the island, and about 18,000 customers lost power at the height of 
the event. In Pembroke Parish, the roof of a former hotel was ripped off, and an exterior wall 
collapsed, which damaged a nearby vehicle (Fig. 11).10

FORECAST AND WARNING CRITIQUE​
 
Genesis 
The genesis of Imelda was anticipated well in advance, although the system formed 
much later than originally forecast (Table 4). The wave from which Imelda developed was 
originally introduced in the 7-day Tropical Weather Outlook (TWO) shortly after it emerged off 
the west coast of Africa, 282 h (11.75 days) prior to genesis. A low 2-day chance of formation

was dropped from the TWO on 18 September. Several days later, the tropical wave was 
reintroduced into the 7-day and 2-day TWO with a low chance of formation 150 h (6.25 days)

Imelda were challenging due to inconsistent global model and ensemble signals as to when 
development might occur, likely related to the hostile environmental conditions that the wave 
encountered within the Main Development Region during the climatological peak of the season. 
A verification of NHC Graphical TWO (GTWO) genesis forecasts for Imelda is shown in 
Figure 12. The struggles to pinpoint the timing of formation also made it difficult to predict 
exactly where Imelda would form, as shown by the 43% overall GTWO hit rate. Early NHC 
genesis forecasts indicated a low chance of formation over the eastern and central tropical 
Atlantic, but this did not occur due to the marginal environmental conditions. After the wave was 
reintroduced into the GTWO, the NHC genesis forecasts generally highlighted the southwestern 
Atlantic Ocean for formation, but most of the medium probability areas were east of where 
Imelda ultimately formed. The high category areas all correctly captured the location of genesis. 
Track 
A verification of NHC official track forecasts (OFCL) for Imelda is given in Table 5a. 
Official track forecast errors were comparable to or lower than the mean official errors for the 
previous 5-yr period from 12–60 h, and greater than the mean errors at 72 h. For the two 
verifying 96-h forecasts, OFCL errors were over 3.5 times larger than the 5-yr means. A 
homogeneous comparison of the official track errors with selected guidance models is given in 
Table 5b. The OFCL forecasts outperformed many of the individual models and consensus aids 
from 12–72 h, although OFCL was generally bested by the UKMET (UKMI) and Google 
DeepMind ensemble (GDMI) during this period. At 96 h, OFCL had larger errors than most of 
the guidance, albeit for only 2 verifying forecasts. 
The close proximity of Humberto and Imelda and the potential for binary interaction 
made early track forecasts very challenging. In particular, there was a bifurcation in the 
long-range track guidance available when advisories were initiated (Fig. 13). While some 
models (GFS, HAFS-A/B, ECMWF) showed a close approach to or even a landfall along the 
southeastern U.S. coast, others (COAMPS-TC and UKMET) favored a sharp eastward turn 
farther offshore. These diverging track solutions were also noted in the various global 
ensembles (not shown). Humberto and pre-Imelda were at roughly the same latitude on 26–27 
September, but Imelda developed more gradually and moved northward more slowly than 
Humberto on 28–30 September (Fig. 14). As a result, Humberto eroded the steering ridge over 
the western Atlantic, which kept Imelda farther offshore than originally forecast. While the official 
NHC forecast never explicitly predicted landfall, early NHC forecasts showed a much closer 
approach to land than what ultimately occurred before trending eastward in response to large 
shifts in the track guidance (Fig. 15). Once the advancing upper-level trough over the eastern 
U.S. became the predominant steering feature (due to the slower storm motion of Imelda), the 
tropical cyclone turned sharply east-northeastward away from the U.S. and accelerated toward

Intensity 
A verification of NHC official intensity forecasts (OFCL) for Imelda is given in Table 6a. 
The NHC intensity forecasts for Imelda were very good, with OFCL errors comparable to the 
5-yr mean at 12 h and lower than the 5-yr means at 24–96 h. Figure 17 shows all of the NHC 
intensity forecasts for Imelda compared to the best track. The official forecasts generally 
captured the periods of strengthening and weakening reasonably well, although Imelda did not 
intensify as quickly as initially predicted. A homogeneous comparison of the official intensity 
errors with selected guidance models is given in Table 6b. The NHC intensity forecasts beat 
almost all of the individual models between 24–96 h, and OFCL bested HCCA, FSSE, and 
GDMI at a majority of verified forecast times. 
Wind Watches and Warnings 
​
Coastal wind watches and warnings associated with Imelda are given in Table 7. The 
NHC initiated potential tropical cyclone advisories at 2100 UTC 26 September on the precursor 
disturbance, which enabled the Bahamas to issue Tropical Storm Watches and Warnings before 
genesis occurred. 
​
For the United States, a Tropical Storm Watch was issued for the east coast of Florida 
from the Palm Beach/Martin County Line to the Flagler/Volusia County Line at 1500 UTC 27 
September. The watch was never upgraded to a warning since subsequent track forecasts 
shifted farther offshore. While no sustained winds of tropical storm force occurred along the 
coast, a few locations within the watch area did report tropical-storm-force gusts on 29–30 
September. 
 
IMPACT-BASED DECISION SUPPORT SERVICES & PUBLIC 
COMMUNICATION​
 
The NHC began formal communication with emergency managers on 25 September 
before the disturbance that became Imelda reached the Bahamas. Thirteen decision support 
briefings were provided to emergency managers and coordinated through the Federal 
Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) Hurricane Liaison Team embedded at the NHC. 
These briefings included video-teleconferences with FEMA HQ, FEMA Region 4, the states of 
South Carolina and North Carolina, and the state and counties of Florida. Briefing support 
continued through 29 September, after which it became clear that Imelda would not threaten  
the United States. In addition, the NHC/TAFB provided seven briefings to the U.S. Coast Guard 
Southeast District from 27–30 September in support of their life-saving mission.
