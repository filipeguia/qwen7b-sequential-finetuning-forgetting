Hurricane Erin (AL052025), 11–22 August 2025

Erin was a very large and long-lived Cabo Verde hurricane that reached category 5 intensity (on the Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale). It moved on a long parabolic track around the North Atlantic Ocean and produced wave and rip current impacts along a large stretch of the Atlantic coastline, causing 13 direct deaths.

Hurricane Erin 11–22 AUGUST 2025

## SYNOPTIC HISTORY

Erin originated from a tropical wave that moved off the west coast of Africa on 8 August. Over the next couple of days, an area of low pressure associated with the wave moved slowly westward to northwestward from the African coast to the Cabo Verde Islands. When the system neared the eastern Cabo Verde Islands, a consolidated area of deep convection formed, and the system acquired a sufficiently well-defined circulation to be considered as a tropical depression while located just southwest of the island of Sal around 0000 UTC 11 August. The system moved slightly north of west and became a tropical storm while making landfall on the island of Santo Antão in the northwestern Cabo Verde Islands by 0600 UTC 11 August. The “best track” chart of Erin’s path is given in Fig. 1, with the wind and pressure histories shown in Figs. 2 and 3, respectively. The best track positions and intensities are listed in Table 12. After initial slight strengthening to an intensity of near 40 kt, Erin moved westward to west-southwestward across the tropical Atlantic from 11 to 13 August with little change in strength, likely due to relatively dry air and marginal sea surface temperatures (SSTs). A well-defined mid-level ridge to the north of the tropical cyclone maintained a mainly westward course during the early part of Erin’s lifetime. The storm had strengthened only a little until 14 August, when the system’s deep convection increased in coverage and organization. By 1200 UTC 15 August, Erin intensified into a hurricane while centered about 375 n mi east of the northernmost Leeward Islands. A slight weakness in the ridge resulted in a turn toward the west-northwest. Late on the 15th and early on the 16th, the hurricane turned back toward the west and, while moving over warmer ocean waters, began to intensify rapidly. Over the 12-hour period from 0600 to 1800 UTC 16 August, Erin’s maximum winds increased by 45 kt and its central pressure deepened by 4.5 mb per hour. The hurricane reached a peak intensity of 140 kt, category 5 intensity on the Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale, with a minimum central pressure of about 913 mb around 1800 UTC that day while its well-defined eye was centered about 80 n mi northeast of Anegada in the northernmost British Virgin Islands. Around that same time, aircraft observations indicated that Erin developed concentric eyewalls, and the hurricane began to weaken later on the 16th. The outer portions of Erin’s southern circulation swept over the British and U.S. Virgin Islands and Puerto Rico, bringing tropical-storm-force winds to some of those areas. On 17 August, while passing a little over 100 n mi north of Puerto Rico, the system turned toward the west-northwest as it headed for a weakness in the subtropical ridge. The eye became less defined on satellite images, and Erin’s maximum winds decreased to near 110 kt. The hurricane turned northwestward on 18 August, and its eye became better defined. Reports from Hurricane Hunter aircraft indicated that the system had re-strengthened to a second peak intensity of near 120 kt around 1200 UTC 18 August, while centered about 90 n mi north-northeast of Grand Turk. The weakening and re-strengthening of the hurricane from 16 to 18 August coincided quite well with the dissipation of the inner eyewall and subsequent eyewall replacement during that period, as illustrated in Fig. 4. Erin caused some gusty winds over the Turks and Caicos Islands and portions of the southeastern Bahamas on 18–19 August.

After reaching its second peak in intensity, Erin turned north-northwestward, moving along the western periphery of a mid-tropospheric subtropical anticyclone. The hurricane weakened on 19 August due to increased westerly vertical wind shear, which resulted in the system’s cloud pattern becoming more asymmetric. Erin’s maximum winds decreased to near 85 kt by 1200 UTC that day. However, the shear soon diminished, the cloud pattern became more symmetric, and the hurricane strengthened again. Erin reached a third intensity peak of 95 kt by 1200 UTC 20 August while it was centered about midway between the Florida peninsula and Bermuda, while its circulation continued to expand. Hurricane Hunter observations indicated that the strongest winds were now located well east of the center. Erin turned northward while continuing to move on the western side of the anticyclone. The system then turned north-northeastward and northeastward with a gradual increase in forward speed on 21 August, passing about 175 n mi southeast of Cape Hatteras around 0600 UTC that day. The outer portion of Erin’s western circulation brought tropical storm conditions to parts of the North Carolina and mid-Atlantic coastline. The cyclone weakened very slowly and accelerated northeastward to east-northeastward as it neared the mid-latitude westerlies. On 22 August, the system’s cloud pattern took on a frontal appearance, and Erin became a hurricane-force extratropical cyclone around 1800 UTC that day. From 23 to 24 August, the system moved even faster toward the northeast over the northern Atlantic while maintaining its hurricane-force intensity. Later on the 24th and on 25-26 August, the cyclone moved much more slowly while making a counterclockwise loop a few hundred miles south of Iceland while weakening. The system merged with another extratropical low between Iceland and Ireland on 27 August.

## METEOROLOGICAL STATISTICS

Observations in Erin (Figs. 2 and 3) include subjective satellite-based Dvorak technique intensity estimates from the Tropical Analysis and Forecast Branch (TAFB) and the Satellite Analysis Branch (SAB), objective Advanced Dvorak Technique (ADT) estimates and Satellite Consensus (SATCON) estimates from the Cooperative Institute for Meteorological Satellite Studies/University of Wisconsin-Madison. Observations also include flight-level and dropwindsonde observations from flights of the 53rd Weather Reconnaissance Squadron of the U.S. Air Force Reserve Command (AFRES) and NOAA. Figure 5 displays the aircraft missions into Erin. There were 24 aircraft missions tasked for Erin, 15 for AFRES and 9 for NOAA including 4 synoptic surveillance missions. Additionally, there were 4 research missions conducted by NOAA aircraft. Data and imagery from NOAA polar-orbiting satellites including the Advanced Microwave Sounding Unit (AMSU), the NASA Global Precipitation Mission (GPM), the European Space Agency’s Advanced Scatterometer (ASCAT), the Defense Meteorological Satellite Program (DMSP) satellites, and the Time-Resolved Observations of Precipitation structure and storm Intensity with a Constellation of Smallsats (TROPICS) satellites, among others, were also useful in constructing the best track of Erin. Erin’s wind field expanded considerably during its trek over the Atlantic, particularly after the cyclone moved into the subtropics. Erin’s size ranged from the 92nd to the 95th percentile of all hurricanes on record in the Atlantic basin north of 20°N latitude. Figure 6 shows the evolution of the tropical-storm-force wind field during the lifetime of Erin. Remarkably, in spite of its huge size, only the outer fringes of Erin’s circulation passed over a few land locations after the system departed the Cabo Verde Islands. Ship reports of winds of tropical storm force associated with Erin are given in Table 2, and selected surface observations from land stations and data buoys are given in Table 3. The highest wind report from a land station was a sustained 53-kt wind with a gust to 64 kt from Anegada, the northernmost of the British Virgin Islands.

Winds and Pressure Erin’s peak intensity of 140 kt at 1800 UTC 16 August is based on a maximum 700 mb flight-level wind of 154 kt from the AFRES at 1639 UTC 16 August. There was a surface (10 m) wind of 179 kt measured by a NOAA dropsonde at around 1403 UTC on the 16th, but this was apparently a gust since the low-level averaged wind from that drop corresponded to a sustained surface wind of around 129 kt. Erin’s minimum central pressure of 913 mb is based on an AFRES dropsonde measurement in the eye of 919 mb around the same time with a surface wind of 59 kt. Tropical-storm-force winds, either sustained or in gusts, were observed over portions of the British and U.S. Virgin Islands, in some locations along the North Carolina, Virginia, Maryland, Delaware, and New Jersey coasts, and on Bermuda. Rainfall and Flooding Two locations on São Vicente in the Cabo Verde Islands, Calhau and Mindelo, measured rainfall totals of 287 mm (11.30 in) and 224 mm (8.82 in) respectively from 0100 to 0800 UTC on 11 August. These rains caused significant freshwater flooding and the drowning deaths of 7 people on São Vicente. Rainfall totals of over 10 inches were observed in the British Virgin Islands (Table 3) with lesser amounts across the other islands of the northeastern Caribbean. Widespread flooding was reported across the British Virgin Islands. Significant impacts included extensive landslides and considerable damage to the road network on Virgin Gorda. While no lives were lost, ten residents sought shelter in Anegada. Storm Surge Erin produced minor storm surge flooding accompanied by damaging waves along the Outer Banks of North Carolina, the shores of the lower Chesapeake Bay, and the Delmarva Peninsula, as well as the shores of the Delaware Bay. Maximum storm surge inundations of 1 to 3 feet above ground level (AGL) were widespread along the coast from North Carolina to New Jersey due to Erin's notably large wave field. Figure 7 shows the National Ocean Service (NOS)

tide station observations along the Mid-Atlantic coast. The NOS tide gauge in Duck, North Carolina, measured a peak water level of 3.4 ft Mean Higher High Water (MHHW). Elsewhere, observations peaked at 3.3 ft MHHW in Wachapreague, Virginia, 3.0 ft MHHW in Lewes, Delaware, and 3.0 ft MHHW in Cape May, New Jersey.

## CASUALTY AND DAMAGE STATISTICS

Thirteen fatalities are directly attributed to Erin3. There were seven deaths due to drownings from freshwater flooding reported on São Vicente in the Cabo Verde Islands. Three persons drowned due to rip currents in Puerto Rico. A person drowned, presumably due to rip currents, at La Romana in the eastern Dominican Republic. There was also one rip current drowning at Sailors Haven on Fire Island, New York, and one rip current drowning at Hampton Beach, New Hampshire. On the North Carolina Outer Banks, extremely large surf on top of storm surge breached dune structures in certain vulnerable areas, covering roads with sand and water. Two structures in Buxton sustained major damage. There was flooding that caused the closure of North Carolina Highway 12 (which runs along the Outer Banks) at Oregon Inlet. The total damage estimate was around $3.1 million from North Carolina, mostly in the community of Buxton in Dare County.

Dozens of people had to be rescued from stranded vehicles and restaurants as floodwaters rose in Margate City on the Jersey Shore.

Impacts to Bermuda were minor, with minimal power outages across the island.

## FORECAST AND WARNING CRITIQUE

Erin’s genesis was not anticipated very far in advance (Table 4), partly because the system developed very far east in the Atlantic basin. The wave from which Erin developed was introduced into the Tropical Weather Outlook (TWO) when it was near the west coast of Africa 48 h prior to genesis with a low (<40%) chance for development within 7 days. The 7-day formation probability was raised to medium (40%-60%) 36 hours prior to the time of genesis and raised to high (>60%) just 18 hours before genesis. The 2-day formation probability was set to low 30 hours prior to genesis and raised to medium only 10.5 hours before genesis, in a special TWO. The 2-day formation probability was never raised to high before the system formed. The location of Erin’s genesis was also not well forecast in the Graphical Tropical Weather Outlooks with the anticipated development area over the tropical Atlantic too far to the west of the actual formation point (Fig. 8). A verification of NHC official track forecasts for Erin, which were mostly quite good, is given in Table 5a. Official track forecast errors were lower than or comparable to the mean official errors for the previous 5-yr period for all forecast intervals. A homogeneous comparison of the official track errors with selected guidance models is given in Table 5b. The HCCA and Google DeepMind (GDMI) guidance were the best performers. Most of the official forecasts were along or slightly east of Erin’s observed track (not shown). A verification of NHC official intensity forecasts for Erin is given in Table 6a. At all forecast periods, the mean official intensity forecast errors were higher than the mean official errors for the previous 5-yr period. The mean climatology and persistence intensity forecast (OCD5) errors for Erin were comparable to the 5-yr averages. A homogeneous comparison of the official intensity errors with selected guidance models is given in Table 6b. For the 24-h through 120-h forecast intervals, the GDMI had the lowest mean errors. The rapid intensification of Erin on 16 August was not very well predicted in the official forecasts displayed in Fig. 9.

Watches and warnings associated with Erin are listed in Table 7. Although the hurricane was not expected to make landfall, the proximity of Erin’s large outer wind field prompted the issuance of Tropical Storm Watches and/or Warnings for portions of the Leeward Islands, the Southeast and Central Bahamas, the North Carolina and Virginia coasts, and Bermuda. The NWS issued storm surge watches and warnings for very exposed portions of the North Carolina coast due to the potential for coastal inundation from Erin. A Storm Surge Watch was first issued at 2100 UTC 18 August from Cape Lookout to Duck, North Carolina. The peak storm surge forecast was 2 to 4 ft AGL. The area within the Storm Surge Watch was upgraded to a Storm Surge Warning at 1500 UTC 19 August. Due to Erin's proximity to the coast, destructive wave action was forecast to cause significant beach erosion, leading to coastal flooding, rendering roads in the Outer Banks of North Carolina impassable. This prompted evacuation decisions in Dare County, North Carolina. The NOS observation at Duck, NC, fell within the forecast range. Outside of the Storm Surge Warning area, the peak storm surge forecast was 1 to 3 feet from Cape Fear, North Carolina, to Montauk Point, New York. Peak water level observations from NOS stations were largely up to 3 ft along the Mid-Atlantic and Northeast coastlines (Fig. 7).

## IMPACT-BASED DECISION SUPPORT SERVICES (IDSS) AND

## PUBLIC COMMUNICATION

NHC began formal communication with emergency managers on Friday, August 15 as Erin was strengthening east of the Caribbean. Five decision support briefings were provided to emergency managers and coordinated through the FEMA Hurricane Liaison Team embedded at the NHC. The briefings were federal video-teleconferences with FEMA HQ and FEMA Region 2, 3, and 4. These briefings continued through Friday, August 21, until Erin started moving away from the United States.
