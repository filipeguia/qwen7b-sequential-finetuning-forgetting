SYNOPTIC HISTORY 
​
​
Mario appears to have originated from a tropical wave that crossed Central America and 
emerged over the eastern Pacific Ocean on 8–9 September. The wave produced disorganized 
showers and thunderstorms while moving westward, and a broad area of low pressure formed 
in association with the wave on 10 September, a couple of hundred n mi south of the coast of 
southern Mexico. The broad low turned northwestward while convective activity increased and 
gradually became better organized. Passive microwave data and satellite images indicate a 
well-defined, low-level circulation center formed early on 11 September, and it is estimated that 
a tropical depression formed by 0600 UTC that day, about 160 n mi southeast of Acapulco, 
Mexico. After briefly moving north-northwestward, the depression turned west-northwestward 
later on 11 September while moving around a low- to mid-level ridge over northern Mexico and 
Texas. The depression strengthened into Tropical Storm Mario by 0000 UTC 12 September, 
about 70 n mi southeast of Acapulco. The “best track” chart of Mario’s path is given in Fig. 1, 
with the wind and pressure histories shown in Figs. 2 and 3, respectively. The best track 
positions and intensities are listed in Table 11. 
Satellite images, surface observations, and microwave data show that the circulation of 
Mario was very small on 12 September while the tropical storm passed near the southwestern 
coast of Mexico (Fig. 4). Convection became less organized later that day due to the negative 
effects of moderate northeasterly shear, dry air entrainment, and the storm’s close proximity to 
land. The small circulation became less defined and elongated in satellite images and 
scatterometer data, and Mario opened into a surface trough by 0000 UTC 13 September. Some 
deep convection flared up along the trough axis that morning while the remnants of Mario 
moved westward and interacted with a mesoscale convective system that moved off the coast of 
west-central Mexico. By midday, scatterometer data revealed the presence of a small but 
well-defined area of low pressure that formed in association with the remnants of Mario. This 
low moved west-southwestward to westward while being steered by a ridge to the north. The 
associated convection was disorganized late on 13 September, but increased in coverage and 
organization early the following morning. Mario became a tropical depression again by 0600 
UTC 14 September, then strengthened into a tropical storm 6 h later when it was located about 
270 n mi south of the southern tip of the Baja California peninsula. 
Mario moved west-northwestward to northwestward during the next couple of days within 
the flow between a deep-layer ridge over northern Mexico and an upper-level trough offshore of 
the Baja California peninsula. Warm waters, weak vertical wind shear, and a moist mid-level

environment supported strengthening on 14–15 September, and Mario reached a peak intensity 
of 55 kt at 1200 UTC 15 September, when it was located about 245 n mi southwest of the 
southern tip of the Baja California peninsula (cover image, Fig. 5). Soon after, Mario

weakening as its deep convection quickly collapsed. Mario degenerated to a 35-kt post-tropical 
low by 1200 UTC 16 September and continued to weaken over the next couple of days while 
moving northwestward and gradually decelerating. The low degenerated to a trough of low 
pressure by 1200 UTC 18 September, roughly 400 n mi west of Cabo San Lazaro, Baja 
California Sur.

METEOROLOGICAL STATISTICS 
​
 ​
Observations in Mario (Figs. 2 and 3) include subjective satellite-based Dvorak 
technique intensity estimates from the Tropical Analysis and Forecast Branch (TAFB) and the 
Satellite Analysis Branch (SAB), objective Advanced Dvorak Technique (ADT) estimates and 
Satellite Consensus (SATCON) estimates from the Cooperative Institute for Meteorological 
Satellite Studies/University of Wisconsin-Madison. Data and imagery from NOAA polar-orbiting 
satellites including the Advanced Microwave Sounding Unit (AMSU), the NASA Global 
Precipitation Mission (GPM), the European Space Agency’s Advanced Scatterometer (ASCAT), 
the Defense Meteorological Satellite Program (DMSP) satellites, and the Time-Resolved 
Observations of Precipitation structure and storm Intensity with a Constellation of Smallsats 
(TROPICS) satellites, among others, were also useful in constructing the best track of Mario. 
There were no reports of sustained tropical-storm-force winds associated with Mario. 
​
Winds and Pressure 
The estimated peak intensity of Mario is 55 kt from 1200–1800 UTC 15 September. This 
is consistent with UW-CIMSS SATCON and AiDT objective estimates during this period, as well 
as a 3.5/55 kt subjective Dvorak classification from TAFB at 1800 UTC that day. Due to the 
relatively large spread in satellite intensity estimates (45–65 kt) on 15 September and a lack of 
scatterometer data around the time of peak intensity, this estimate is more uncertain than usual. 
The estimated minimum central pressure of 994 mb is based on the Knaff-Zehr-Courtney (KZC) 
pressure-wind relationship. 
As Mario passed near the coast of southern Mexico, the Acapulco International Airport 
(MMAA) reported a peak sustained wind of 22 kt and a gust of 32 kt at 0315 UTC 12 
September. A couple of days later, an automated Mexican Navy weather station on Socorro 
Island recorded a peak wind gust of 29 kt at 1730 UTC 14 September and a minimum sea level 
pressure of 1001.6 mb just before 0000 UTC 15 September as Mario passed near the island. 
The following day, an inland weather station at Ciudad Constitución in Baja California Sur 
reported a sustained wind of 21 kt and a gust of 37 kt at 1610 UTC 15 September while Mario 
passed well offshore.

Rainfall and Flooding 
Mario and its remnants contributed to an extended period of rainfall that occurred across 
portions of southwestern and west-central Mexico and the Baja California peninsula from 11–16 
September (Fig. 6). The heaviest rainfall occurred on 12–13 September when Mario made its 
closest approach to the southwestern coast of Mexico, and a couple of stations in Guerrero and 
Colima reported more than 6 inches (~150 mm) of rainfall during the 24-h period. Media reports2 
indicate some localized flooding occurred in parts of Guerrero, including Acapulco and 
Zihuatanejo. 
Maximum reported Mexico rainfall totals by state from 11–16 September: 
Guerrero: 9.06 inches (230 mm) at Laguna de Coyuca, Coyuca de Benítez​
​
Nayarit: 8.58 inches (218 mm) at Gaviotas, Bahía de Banderas​
​
Colima: 7.06 inches (179.2 mm) at P. deriv. Gregorio T. Q. Jala, Tecomán​
​
Jalisco: 5.79 inches (147 mm) at Cajón de Peña, Tomatlán​
​
Michoacán: 4.56 inches (115.8 mm) at Observatorio de Zamora, Zamora​
 
After Mario degenerated to a remnant low, a large plume of residual mid-level moisture 
was advected northward by an offshore trough during the ensuing few days. Moist ascent over 
the mountains of the Baja California peninsula resulted in heavy rainfall that produced flash and 
river flooding in parts of Baja California Sur on 16–17 September, most notably affecting the 
town of San Ignacio. This lingering moisture also contributed to flooding rains across portions of 
the southwestern United States on 17–18 September. 
​
CASUALTY AND DAMAGE STATISTICS​
 
There were no direct fatalities3 associated with Mario as a tropical cyclone. Media 
reports4 indicate one indirect fatality occurred on 16 September in San Ignacio, Baja California 
Sur, where a 37-year-old man died by electrocution during flooding. There are no official 
damage estimates available at the time of this report. 
No major damage was reported in the state of Guerrero, where Mario made its closest 
approach to the coast of Mexico. News articles indicate that gusty winds downed some trees 
and power poles in Acapulco, Zihuatanejo, and surrounding areas, which resulted in brief power 
and communication outages. Some landslides were also reported in Acapulco. More significant 
damage occurred in parts of the Mulegé and La Paz municipalities of Baja California Sur as a

FORECAST AND WARNING CRITIQUE​
 
Genesis 
The first genesis of Mario was not forecast well in advance (Table 2a). The incipient 
wave was introduced in the Tropical Weather Outlook (TWO) only 72 h prior to formation, and it 
reached the medium and high categories 60 h and 54 h before genesis, respectively. The 
disturbance was introduced with a low chance of development in the 2-day TWO 60 h before 
genesis. The 2-day probabilities were raised to the medium and high categories 36 h and 12 h 
before formation, respectively. In general, the global models did not provide a very strong signal 
for genesis until only a few days before Mario formed. 
The second genesis of Mario was not anticipated (Table 2b). The remnants of Mario 
were introduced with a low chance of formation in the 7-day (2-day) TWO only 18 h (12 h) prior 
to genesis. The 2-day probabilities did not reach the medium or high category before genesis 
was determined to have occurred in post analysis. Forecaster confidence for genesis was low 
because there were multiple vorticity centers present (including the remnants of Mario) within a 
broader gyre well south of Baja California. From the available model guidance, it was unclear 
whether these features would consolidate or remain separate, weaker features embedded 
within the broader circulation. Ultimately, the redevelopment that occurred was from the 
low-level vorticity associated with the remnants of Mario.  
The location of Mario’s initial genesis was accurately forecast (Fig. 7), with all of the 
formation areas capturing the region where Mario formed. The genesis areas for Mario’s 
redevelopment were also accurate (Fig. 8), albeit with limited lead time. 
Track 
A verification of NHC official track forecasts for Mario is given in Table 3a. Official track 
forecast errors were lower than the mean official errors for the previous 5-yr period at 12–24 h 
and 72 h, but greater than the 5-yr means at 36–60 h and 96 h. Note that the forecast sample 
size is relatively small and irregularly distributed. This is due to a couple of factors: (1) Mario had 
a fairly short existence as a tropical cyclone, and (2) Mario briefly dissipated and is shown as a 
disturbance or low for about a day in the final best track. Current  NHC verification practice only 
includes forecasts if the system was classified in the final best track as a tropical cyclone at both 
the forecast’s initial time and at the projection’s valid time. 
A homogeneous comparison of the official track errors with selected guidance models is 
given in Table 3b. Since some models had difficulty tracking Mario due to its small size and/or 
showed it dissipating inland over Mexico, there are several models that did not have a large

Intensity 
A verification of NHC official intensity forecasts for Mario is given in Table 4a. Official 
intensity forecast errors were lower than the mean official errors for the previous 5-yr period at 
all forecast times, albeit for a relatively small sample size. A homogeneous comparison of the 
official intensity errors with available guidance models is given in Table 4b. The 12–36-h NHC 
intensity forecasts were better than almost every available model, but several models and 
consensus aids had lower errors at 48–96 h. Early NHC forecasts had a high bias and showed 
Mario becoming a hurricane, which ultimately did not occur. 
Wind Watches and Warnings 
​
A Tropical Storm Watch was issued by the government of Mexico at 1500 UTC 12 
September for the southwestern coast of Mexico from Lazaro Cardenas to Punta San Telmo. 
The watch was never upgraded to a warning, and it was discontinued at 0300 UTC 13 
September.
